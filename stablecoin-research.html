01

The question behind every consortium

Open USD went live on 30 September. Coinbase, Mastercard, Shopify, Stripe and Visa each hold an equal initial equity stake in the company behind it. Headlines called it a Circle killer. That reading is too narrow.

The deeper story is about where the money sits.

The classic model

Users
Platforms
Issuer
Reserve income

In the classic model, reserve income largely accrues to the issuer. The issuer earns the interest on the reserves. The platforms that bring the users earn almost nothing.

“

A stablecoin is a distribution business with an issuer-shaped profit pool.

Every consortium on the map is an attempt to move that pool, or to stop it from leaving the building. The difference between them is who sits at the table and what they want back.

02

Three business models

Each consortium answers the same question in a different way. The dot colours on the map match the cards below.

Archetype 01

Network economics

Examples

Open USD Global Dollar Network (USDG)

A single regulated issuer does the plumbing. Partners who bring volume get paid, in rewards or equity. This is a customer acquisition programme dressed as a coin.

Strength

The partners already own the rails: checkout, cards, exchanges, wallets.

Weakness

It is still one issuer under the hood. The shared part is the economics, not the balance sheet.

Archetype 02

Bank-owned issuer

Examples

Qivalis 21-bank USD group Japan’s megabanks Zelle’s ZLUSD Korea’s planned consortium

Here the banks own the issuer. The motive is defensive. If customers hold stablecoins at a platform instead of deposits at a bank, the bank loses its cheapest funding. A token owned by many banks can be accepted by all of them.

Strength

Banks own trust and customers, and one pooled token works across all of them.

Weakness

Banks own customers but not the crypto venues where stablecoins circulate. Competitors must also agree on governance before they can ship.

Archetype 03

Licensed joint venture

Examples

AllUnity Anchorpoint DDSC

Two or three specialists combine what none has alone: a licence, a bank balance sheet, distribution, technology.

Strength

The easiest model to execute. Small groups decide fast and the regulator knows who to call.

Weakness

Most of these tokens are local currencies, which tend to stay small.

Hedging, not choosing

Wells Fargo sits in the 21-bank stablecoin group, among Zelle’s owners, in The Clearing House and on Swift’s ledger. Banks are not picking between stablecoins and tokenized deposits. They are buying a seat in both.

Euro and dollar tables overlap

BBVA and Rabobank sit in both Qivalis and the 21-bank USD group. BNP Paribas left the 21-bank group and kept its seat at Qivalis.

The bank that is not there

JPMorgan, which already runs a live tokenized deposit product, sits among Zelle’s owners and in The Clearing House. It is not in the 21-bank stablecoin group.

The question is not bank or network. It is how many tables one bank can afford to sit at.

04

The other path: tokenized deposits

Not every bank group is building a stablecoin. Many are building the other thing: tokenized deposits.

A tokenized deposit is an ordinary bank deposit in digital form. It stays a claim on the bank, with the same insurance and rules as before.

Stablecoin versus tokenized depositStablecoinTokenized deposit
What you hold

A claim on a reserve pool held by the issuer. Qivalis, for example, plans to back each coin 1:1 with euros and high-quality liquid assets.

A claim on the issuing bank. The funds stay a deposit at that bank.

Where the money sits

In reserves outside the bank. Bank of England Governor Andrew Bailey has warned that stablecoins take money out of the banking system.

On the bank’s balance sheet, so the bank keeps its funding and its ability to lend.

Protection

Reserve and licensing rules instead: the GENIUS Act in the US, MiCA in the EU.

The same deposit insurance and prudential rules as an ordinary deposit.

Who can use it

Any wallet or exchange on the chain. Open USD runs on Ethereum, Solana, Base and Tempo.

Participating banks and their verified clients. The Clearing House network is restricted to supervised institutions and their verified clients.

A shared ledger that matches and nets bank-issued tokenized deposits before final settlement through existing systems.

Why banks like it

A tokenized deposit keeps the money on the bank’s balance sheet. A stablecoin can pull it out.

Where it falls short

It only moves between participating banks. It does not reach the exchanges and wallets where stablecoins already circulate.

05

Why they exist now

Three forces arrived together.

01

Regulation made it safe to build.

Hong Kong, the EU and the US now have licensing frameworks. A bank cannot commit balance sheet to a legal grey zone. A clear regime turns a research project into a product.

January 2027

The US GENIUS Act takes effect, or 120 days after final rules if that comes first.

02

Nobody wants to build on a competitor.

A payment company will not route its volume through a token owned by a rival. A consortium turns a competitor’s product into neutral infrastructure.

Money is political.

Euro, yen and won groups exist partly because dollar tokens dominate.

07

What comes next

01

Overlap and shake-out

Banks are hedging. Scotiabank and TD sit in the 21-bank stablecoin group and in Canada’s separate tokenized deposit project. Expect some memberships to be quietly dropped as the groups harden into real companies with real costs.

02

Distribution decides the winners

In the network model, the question is whether partners route volume through the coin or just attach a logo. Bank groups face the opposite problem: they own trust and customers but have to rent the venues. Whoever solves distribution first wins.

03

The calendar sorts the field

Slow groups will meet a market where Open USD already has liquidity and habits.

04

Consortia will connect to each other

A bank group will not ignore a network with that much volume. Expect banks to join network models as distributors while building their own tokens. The lines between archetypes will blur.

08

The bottom line

The consortium wave is not about who builds the better coin. It is about who earns the yield and who owns the customer relationship.

For an institution, the choice is now about which seat to take: owner, distributor or user.

Seat 01

Owner

Owners carry the cost and the governance.

Seat 02

Distributor

Distributors earn on volume.

Seat 03

User

Users get cheaper rails with little control.

Most institutions will end up in more than one seat.
